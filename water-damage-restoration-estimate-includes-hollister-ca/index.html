What a Water Damage Restoration Estimate Should Include in Hollister, CA

A checklist for reading an estimate so you can compare plans, not just totals.

Call (831) 271-0459

By the Hollister Water Damage Pros team. Published and reviewed November 10, 2026.

A good estimate shows what will be done, where, with what and for how long. A weak one gives a total and little else. After a water loss you are making fast decisions, so it helps to know what a clear estimate looks like before you need one.

The basic parts of an estimate

SectionWhat it should say
Property and loss detailsService address, date of loss, source of water, water category
Scope of workRooms, materials to remove, steps and phases
EquipmentTypes and quantities of air movers, dehumidifiers and other tools
DurationExpected number of drying days and monitoring visits
ExclusionsWhat is not included, such as rebuild, permits or plumbing repairs
Pricing basisHow price is set, for example by line item

Use the street address on the estimate. Hollister has two ZIP codes, 95023 for street addresses and 95024 for post office boxes, so make sure the service location is correct.

Why equipment and days matter

Drying is the largest part of many restoration bills, and it depends on how many pieces of equipment run and for how many days. An estimate that lists the equipment and an expected duration makes it possible to compare plans. A flat price with no details can hide a plan that uses too little equipment, or too much.

Ask how the number of days will be decided and what happens if readings show more time is needed. A good answer is that readings decide, and the crew will tell you before extending the plan. See structural drying for how readings work.

What affects the total

  • Water category, from clean to contaminated.
  • Square footage and number of rooms affected.
  • Materials involved, such as carpet, hardwood, drywall and insulation.
  • Equipment count and drying days.
  • Rebuild scope, which is often priced separately.

Items that are often left out

  • Permits and inspections.
  • Plumbing, roofing or electrical repairs.
  • Contents cleaning or storage.
  • Painting, texture and finish carpentry.
  • Hidden damage found after walls are opened.

None of these are surprises if the estimate lists them as exclusions. They become surprises when nobody mentions them. For a larger loss, ask whether rebuild is part of the estimate or handled in a separate phase, and whether an itemized breakdown is available.

Comparing estimates fairly

Compare the same scope. If one estimate includes drywall removal and another does not, the totals are not comparable. Put the line items side by side, and ask each company to explain differences. If you are comparing a restoration company with a plumber or contractor, remember that each covers a different part of the job.

An example of how to compare two estimates

ItemEstimate AEstimate B
Water category statedYesNo

Drying days and monitoring visitsThree days, daily readingsNot stated
Materials removed and heightsDrywall to two feetAs needed
Exclusions listedRebuild, permits, plumbingNone

The totals may look similar, but Estimate A tells you what you are buying and Estimate B does not. When a number is hard to compare, ask the company to explain it in terms of equipment, days and materials.

Questions about payment

  • When is payment due, and does it depend on the insurer?
  • Is a deposit required, and what does it cover?
  • How are changes to the scope billed?
  • What documents come with the final invoice?

Be wary of any estimate that asks for full payment before any work is done. A clear payment schedule tied to stages of the work is a better sign.

Why we avoid quoting a price on the phone

Water damage depends on how far the water traveled, what it touched and how long it sat. A number given without seeing the space is a guess, and a guess that is too low turns into a change order later. We prefer to look at readings and then explain the scope and price before work starts.

That approach also gives you something to compare. When you have two scopes with equipment and days written out, the difference in price becomes a question about the plan rather than a mystery.

Two Hollister ZIP codes and why they matter on paper

USPS data lists 95023 as a standard ZIP for Hollister and 95024 as a post office box ZIP for Hollister. For a restoration estimate, an insurance form or a permit application, the street address should use the standard ZIP for the property.

If you are in the nearby communities of Tres Pinos or San Juan Bautista, your ZIP codes are 95075 and 95045, and an estimate should use the correct one for the service address.

Questions homeowners ask

Should an estimate be itemized?

An itemized estimate makes comparison easier, and it is useful for insurance. Ask for the scope, equipment and days.

Can the price change after work starts?

It can if hidden damage is found or readings show more drying is needed. A good company explains this and tells you before changing the plan.

Why do estimates vary so much?

Different scopes, equipment counts and assumptions about days. Compare line items, not just totals.

Which ZIP code should I use on a Hollister estimate?

Use the ZIP for your street address, which is typically 95023. The 95024 ZIP is used for post office boxes.
